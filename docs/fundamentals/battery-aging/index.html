Why does a lithium-ion cell wear out?

A cell loses performance gradually, through unwanted side reactions and physical changes in its materials. The damage is essentially irreversible and eventually ends in failure. This lesson takes that sentence apart: six mechanisms, where each one happens, what speeds it up, and which of three observable signals it feeds — impedance up, capacity down, self-discharge up.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • name six aging mechanisms and say where in the cell each one happens;
  • link each mechanism to impedance rise, capacity fade or self-discharge;
  • use the Arrhenius factor to estimate how temperature changes a degradation rate;
  • explain why charging a cold cell makes the temperature–aging curve U-shaped.

Before you start: Part 01B · Cell architecture for the electrode sandwich, and Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate for what “2C” means.

What the numbers are

The equations here are teaching models — a √t film, Arrhenius factors, one-line stress terms. They reproduce the direction, the shape and the rough relative size of each effect. They are not a state-of-health estimator, and no number on this page should be quoted for a real cell: quote the paper it came from.

01The whole chain on one page

Six mechanisms. Three effects. One outcome.

The mechanisms are side reactions and physical changes, and not every chemistry suffers from all of them. Their observable consequences fall into three useful signals: impedance rises, capacity falls and self-discharge rises. Other diagnostic quantities change too; these three are the simplified outputs the teaching model uses. Select a mechanism to trace where it ends up.

Where this comes from — Vetter et al. 2005 (the canonical taxonomy of these mechanisms) · Edge et al. 2021 (how they interact) · Birkl et al. 2017 (why they collapse into three measurable effects).

02Where it happens

The panel shows a zoomed cross-section of one repeating electrode sandwich: copper current collector, porous graphite negative electrode, separator, porous NMC positive electrode, aluminium current collector. Electrolyte fills every pore of the three middle layers — the structure from Part 01B.

The most common misconception is that these mechanisms happen in separate places. They do not. They happen on particle surfaces, inside particles and at material interfaces, distributed through the whole cell. In a pouch cell these sandwiches are stacked; in a cylindrical cell they are wound.

Where this comes from — Plett 2015, Vol. I for the porous-electrode picture · Vetter et al. 2005 for where each mechanism sits · geometry after Argonne’s battery fundamentals.

The rate law underneath all of it

Almost every mechanism here is a chemical reaction, and chemical reaction rates follow the Arrhenius law:

k(T)k(Tref)=exp[EaR(1Tref−1T)]

For a typical activation energy near 50 kJ/mol this gives the familiar rule of thumb: the rate roughly doubles for every +10 °C near room temperature. Within one thermally activated mechanism, heat usually accelerates the same reaction; across a real cell, temperature can also change which mechanism dominates.

03Passivation — the SEI film

Graphite negative electrodes suffer a particular form of corrosion called passivation. The electrolyte solvent is not chemically stable at the potentials found in most lithium-ion cells, so it reacts with the graphite particles. The reaction products form a layer on the particle surfaces: the solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI).

That film is not purely bad news. It protects the graphite from further reaction, which is why the process slows so sharply after the first few cycles. But it never stops: the SEI keeps growing slowly for the whole life of the cell, and every nanometre of growth consumes lithium that can no longer be cycled. Two ideas do most of the work:

  • √t growth. The film is its own diffusion barrier, so its thickness grows like the square root of time, not linearly.
  • Arrhenius. Warmer means faster — roughly double per +10 °C.
δ(t,T)=δ1t·k(T)k(25 °C)

In the teaching model, δ1 is 12 nm of film after one year at 25 °C, and capacity loss is proportional to film thickness. Set the panel to 45 °C and watch what a hot summer does to a cell that is doing nothing at all.

04Gas generation

Some chemistries produce gaseous products while charging that, ideally, return to their earlier state on discharge. Two quite different failure paths come out of that one reaction:

  • The gas escapes. Through a breach in the enclosure, that capacity is gone for good — and in some cells the released gases are flammable or explosive. Overcharged lead-acid cells, for example, give off oxygen and hydrogen.
  • The gas stays. In a sealed cell, pressure builds until the cell swells or ruptures unless it has a release vent. A vent makes the failure survivable, not free: venting loses electrolyte too.

The model balances generation against recombination, so the gas held in the cell approaches a plateau:

V(t)=gr(1−e−rt)

Within specification the plateau stays low. Push generation g up with heat or overcharge and the plateau climbs past swelling, then past the vent. Move the overcharge slider and watch the two thresholds arrive in that order.

Where this comes from — Rowden & Garcia-Araez 2020, a review of which gases form, when, and what they do · Vetter et al. 2005 for electrolyte decomposition.

05Crystal formation

Material that leaves an electrode during discharge does not generally return to the same place when the cell is recharged. Instead, crystal structures tend to build up on the electrode surfaces. This is a very general mechanism in redox cells, and its consequence is geometric rather than chemical.

A porous electrode works because it has an enormous internal surface area — that is where the reaction happens. As crystals accumulate, the active area shrinks and the reaction has to squeeze through a smaller doorway. Resistance rises, so the cell loses its ability to deliver high power. This is a power problem before it is a capacity problem.

06Dendrites — crystal growth that becomes a safety problem

Tree-like metal structures can grow through the separator, raising the self-discharge rate or even causing a short circuit. In lithium-ion cells the trigger is specific and well understood: charging at low temperature or with excessive current can deposit lithium metal on the negative-electrode particles instead of inserting it into them — lithium plating. Some deposits develop filament-like structures that threaten the separator.

Why cold makes it worse is worth stating plainly. Intercalation is a kinetic process, so its rate falls with temperature like every other reaction. The charging current, however, is whatever the charger decides to push. When lithium arrives faster than the graphite can take it in, it has nowhere to go but onto the surface, as metal.

The heat map is the practical summary: the danger is the top-left corner — high current, low temperature. That is why a well-designed battery management system refuses to fast-charge a cold pack, and warms it first if it can.

07Volume change, cracking and loss of contact

Charging and discharging an intercalation electrode changes its volume, and that stress can crack the active material. This is a primarily mechanical mechanism, and one that responds strongly to how you cycle; temperature, materials, particle design and electrode construction matter too. Every insertion and removal of lithium swells and shrinks the host lattice. Repeat it a few thousand times and the particle behaves like any other fatigued material.

It damages the cell in two ways at once:

  • Fracture and structural collapse take active material out of service → capacity fade. In some positive-electrode materials the structure collapses so far that it can no longer intercalate lithium at all.
  • Broken binder and conductive additive electrically orphan particles that are still chemically fine → impedance rise.

Three results are worth pulling out by hand:

  • Shelf storage at 25 °C is dominated by passivation, and takes over a decade to reach 80 %.
  • Hot operation compresses that to about two years, without the cell doing anything different.
  • Cold fast-charging hands the blame to plating instead — a different mechanism, a different warning sign, and a safety problem rather than a performance one.

One caveat the panel cannot show: measured capacity can appear to recover slightly after rest, temperature equilibration or a different diagnostic procedure. That does not reverse the underlying degradation, and deep discharge should not be used as a general rejuvenation method.

Where this comes from — Birkl et al. 2017 on separating the modes from measurable behaviour · Han et al. 2019 for whole-life-cycle behaviour · Attia et al. 2022 on why the trajectory can bend sharply downward (“knees”).

09Temperature shifts the aging balance

Higher temperature generally accelerates aging, so keeping cells inside an acceptable temperature range is the most effective single way to extend their life. It is the most actionable idea in the whole topic, and the panel below shows why.

The catch — and the reason the total curve is U-shaped rather than simply rising — is lithium plating. Cooling a cell slows the side reactions, but it also slows the intercalation you want, so a cold cell that is being charged plates lithium instead. Cold is only free if no current is flowing. Switch between shelf storage and cold / fast charge to see both halves. Both curves come from the model in section 08, so the U-shape is a result, not a second opinion.

Three sentences worth keeping
  1. Aging is gradual and essentially irreversible, and it ends in failure. Rest or a different diagnostic procedure can show an apparent short-term recovery, but it does not undo degradation.
  2. This teaching model summarises aging through impedance ↑, capacity ↓ and self-discharge ↑.
  3. Temperature strongly changes degradation rates, while cold charging adds a distinct plating risk.

11Check yourself

Work these out from the panels above before opening the answers.

The SEI film is a degradation product. Why is a cell with no SEI at all worse off?

Because the film is what stops the reaction. Without a passivating layer the solvent would keep reacting with the graphite at its initial, very fast rate. The SEI is the reason the loss curve bends over into √t instead of continuing as a straight line — a self-limiting side reaction is enormously better than an unlimited one. In section 03, compare the film after one year with the film after four.

A pack is fast-charged at 0 °C every day. Which mechanism dominates, and which effect shows up first?

Lithium plating. In section 06, set 0 °C and 2–3C: the teaching model shows a much larger plating drive than at 25 °C. Irreversible capacity loss is a common early consequence when plated lithium becomes electrically isolated. Increased self-discharge is a more serious possible symptom if deposits create leakage paths or a soft internal short; it is not guaranteed to appear first.

Two identical cells sit on a shelf, one at 25 °C and one at 45 °C. Roughly how much faster does the hot one age?

About 3.5× faster, from the Arrhenius factor for a reaction near 50 kJ/mol — doubling per 10 °C, and 20 °C is two doublings plus a bit. In section 08, set shelf storage and compare: time to 80 % goes from over a decade to a couple of years, with the cell doing no work at all.

Why is a rising self-discharge rate treated as more alarming than a slow capacity fade?

Because an unexpected rise can indicate a leakage path or soft internal short and therefore deserves investigation. Self-discharge can also come from parasitic reactions or electronic leakage without separator failure, so it is a warning signal, not proof of one specific mechanism.

Your pack lives in a hot climate and is fast-charged. You can fix one thing. Which?

Keep the cells inside their validated operating window and manage charge power with temperature. For the illustrative cold / fast charge duty, the model favours the low twenties over 45 °C. For a pack that is already cold, reduce the charge current or pre-heat it — extra cooling would raise the plating risk.
