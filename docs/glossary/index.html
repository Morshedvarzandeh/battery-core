Ampere-hour (Ah)

A unit of electric charge: one ampere flowing for one hour. Battery capacity is usually rated in ampere-hours, which measure charge, not energy.

Learn it in Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate →
Arrhenius law

The rule that a thermally activated reaction speeds up exponentially with temperature, set by its activation energy. Near room temperature many degradation reactions roughly double their rate for every +10 °C.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
C-rate

Current divided by nominal capacity, in reciprocal hours. 1C on a 20 Ah cell is 20 A; 2C is 40 A. C-rate lets you compare loads on cells of different sizes.

Learn it in Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate →
Calendar aging

Degradation that happens with time even when a cell is not cycled — in storage, for example. It is driven mainly by side reactions such as SEI growth and accelerates with temperature.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Calendering

Compressing a dried electrode coating between rollers to set its thickness, density and porosity. It is one of the electrode-making steps in cell production.

Learn it in Part 03 · Battery production →
Capacity fade

The loss of charge a cell can store and deliver, for example through lithium locked into the SEI or active material that has cracked out of service.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Current collector

The thin metal foil that carries electrons between an electrode and the external circuit — usually copper on the negative side and aluminium on the positive side.

Learn it in Part 01B · Cell architecture →
Cutoff voltage

The terminal-voltage limit at which a charge or discharge is stopped. A real discharge ends at the cutoff voltage, which is why actual runtime can differ from the ideal duration.

Learn it in Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate →
Dendrite

A tree-like metal deposit that can grow from the negative electrode through the separator, raising self-discharge or causing an internal short circuit.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Depth of discharge (DOD)

How much of a cell’s capacity is used in a cycle. Deep cycles cause more mechanical stress in the active materials than shallow ones.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Diffusion coefficient

The proportionality constant D in Fick’s first law, in m²/s. It sets how fast a species spreads for a given concentration gradient.

Learn it in Fick’s first law →
Electrolyte

The ion-conducting medium between the electrodes. In a liquid-electrolyte cell it fills the pores of both electrodes and the separator, so it is not a separate structural layer.

Learn it in Part 01B · Cell architecture →
Fick’s first law

J = −D dc/dx: diffusive flux is proportional to the concentration gradient and points down it.

Learn it in Fick’s first law →
Formation

The first controlled charge and discharge cycles of a newly assembled cell, during which the SEI forms. It is part of cell production, before aging and grading.

Learn it in Part 03 · Battery production →
Ideal duration

t = 1 / C-rate: how long a constant current would take to transfer the full nominal capacity. A definition, not a prediction of real runtime.

Learn it in Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate →
Impedance rise

An increase in a cell’s internal resistance as it ages. It costs voltage under load and therefore power, even while the stored charge is still there.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Intercalation

Lithium inserting into, and leaving, the host structure of an electrode material — between graphite layers, for example — without destroying it.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Lithium plating

Lithium metal depositing on the negative-electrode surface instead of intercalating into it. It is promoted by charging at low temperature or high current.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Nominal capacity

The charge a cell is rated to deliver under specified test conditions — current, temperature, rest and voltage limits — usually in ampere-hours.

Learn it in Part 02 · Capacity and C-rate →
Passivation

The formation of a surface film that slows further reaction. In lithium-ion cells, the SEI passivates the graphite negative electrode.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Porous electrode

An electrode made of active-material particles, conductive additive and binder with electrolyte-filled pores between them. Its large internal surface is where the reaction happens.

Learn it in Part 01B · Cell architecture →
Self-discharge

The slow loss of stored charge while a cell is not in use. An unexpected rise can signal a leakage path or a soft internal short.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
Separator

A porous, electronically insulating film between the electrodes. Electrons cannot cross it; ions move through the electrolyte in its pores.

Learn it in Part 01 · Cell anatomy →
Slurry

The mixture of active material, conductive carbon, binder and solvent that is coated onto a current-collector foil to make an electrode.

Learn it in Part 03 · Battery production →
Solid electrolyte

A solid ion conductor that replaces the liquid electrolyte in an all-solid-state cell. Oxide, sulfide, halide and polymer families each need a different production route.

Learn it in Part 03B · Solid-state production →
Solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI)

A film of electrolyte reaction products on the negative-electrode particles. It protects the graphite from further reaction but grows slowly for the whole life of the cell, consuming cyclable lithium.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
State of charge (SOC)

How full a cell is, as a fraction of its available capacity. Storage temperature and state of charge both influence calendar aging.

Learn it in Part 04 · Battery aging →
State of health (SOH)

A summary of how far a cell has aged, often expressed through remaining capacity or increased resistance compared with when it was new.
